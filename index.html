02 · The Gap

Two customer bases Recurly can't see today

  • RevRec today is a feature of Recurly Subscriptions. If a contract's billing event doesn't originate inside Recurly, RevRec never learns about it - regardless of how strategically important that customer is.
  • Most B2B contract revenue never touches Recurly's billing engine. Multi-element deals, CPQ-sourced invoices, marketplace revenue - invisible to a billing-coupled RevRec.
  • The market for "gets the schedule right" is bigger than "bills the transaction." That escalation path runs to the CFO regardless of whose platform is upstream.

B2B SaaS with its own billing stack

Companies that run CPQ and invoicing through Salesforce, NetSuite, or a homegrown system. Multi-element arrangements - license plus services plus usage - are exactly where manual SSP allocation breaks down.

Multi-source revenue businesses

Merchants whose revenue lands in more than one system - Stripe here, a marketplace there, an ERP everywhere else. A billing-coupled RevRec sees one slice and calls it complete.

The trust stake. A finance team that can't close the books on schedule loses credibility with the board before it loses anything else. That escalation path runs to the CFO regardless of whose platform is upstream - which means the market for "gets the schedule right" is bigger than the market for "bills the transaction."

04 · Competitive Landscape

Five postures, not five feature lists

Knowing which posture a competitor has chosen says more than knowing their feature set - it says where Recurly should and shouldn't compete.

Zuora - the deep engine, decoupled from the ERP

Enterprise benchmark for contract complexity. Keeps recognition logic out of the ERP entirely, preserving GL governance and SOX-grade audit trails. Wins on depth; a decade of head start.

Stripe - commoditize it as an attach product

0.25% of recognized revenue, no upfront cost - priced to be a rounding error next to processing fees. Technically shallow: treats invoices, not contracts, as the recognition driver.

Chargebee - monetization complexity as the wedge

Every pricing innovation (usage, credits, ramps) becomes a recognition obligation Finance wasn't built for. RevRec is one of seven bundled modules.

Maxio - vertical specialist, deliberately narrow

Purpose-built for $1M–$100M ARR B2B software. Proof that a narrow ICP is defensible even against much larger platforms.

The unbundlers - RightRev, Leapfin, LedgerUp, Ordway

Plug into whatever billing exists; aggregate every revenue source into one governed subledger. The real structural threat, since merchants rarely have all revenue in one place.

Where the wedge sits

Contract complexity against coupling to a single billing system

Mid-complexity, source-agnostic, lifecycle-native - open ground between the unbundlers and Zuora.
05 · Why Recurly, Why Now

The differentiation isn't depth. It's lifecycle fidelity.

  • Recurly won't out-allocate Zuora on SSP edge cases with a decade less experience. Competing on Zuora's terms is a losing bet regardless of engineering investment.
  • It already runs the one primitive every unbundler struggles with - lifecycle-event-to-schedule adjustment, millions of times a day, for consumer subscriptions. That's the hard part.
  • The bet: generalize the lifecycle-to-schedule engine into an ingestion-agnostic layer. Compete on "gets the event right," not "handles every allocation edge case."
  • Every upgrade, downgrade, pause, and cancellation already generates a schedule adjustment inside Recurly's engine - millions of times a day, for consumer subscriptions.
  • That's the hard part, not a side effect. LedgerUp's own positioning: most deferred-revenue errors originate in mismatched contract and billing data, not in the recognition logic itself.

  • Recurly already has the playbook. Justt.ai (chargeback dispute management) shows Recurly owning the workflow and data layer while partnering for a specialized engine - the same shape applies here.
Own what merchants judge you on and what compounds over time. Rent what's commodity infrastructure backed by network effects you could never replicate.

What I'd be looking for
  • Artifacts, not adjectives. Ask them to screen-share the actual close checklist and the reconciliation workbook. Count the tabs, the manual journal entries, the people in the thread. "It's painful" is not data; a 40-tab workbook is.
  • Money already moving. Are they paying a contractor, a Big Four advisory team, or a point tool today? An existing budget line is proven willingness to pay and beats every stated intention.
  • The trigger event. Nobody buys revenue infrastructure on a quiet Tuesday. Look for the pattern: audit finding, restatement, IPO readiness, new CFO, ERP migration, or launching a new pricing model. The trigger determines the GTM motion.
  • The exception ranking. Which lifecycle events generate the most manual adjustments, ordered by volume. A direct roadmap input - and the seed for the eval set in Section 10.
  • Who signs, and what loses. Whose budget, what approval threshold, and what else that money would have gone to.
Questions I'd ask, and the ones I wouldn't

Ask these

  • Walk me through last month's close, hour by hour, from cutoff to sign-off.
  • Show me the last five manual journal entries and tell me what caused each one.
  • The last time you evaluated a tool for this - what happened, and why did it end where it did?
  • What did your auditor push back on most recently?
  • If close took three days instead of ten, what would you personally do with the time?

Never ask these

  • "Would you buy this?" - everyone is polite in a discovery call.
  • "How much would you pay?" - an unanchored number is worse than no number.
  • "Would this feature be useful?" - everything is useful when it's free.

And don't demo first. A demo anchors the buyer and contaminates every willingness-to-pay answer that follows.

The one question the whole thesis rests on. When a revenue accountant ranks their sources of manual work, does contract-change propagation come out on top, or does SSP allocation judgment? If fewer than half name propagation, the lifecycle wedge is wrong and the build order flips toward allocation logic. I'd rather find that out in week three than in quarter three.
Kill criteria
  • No existing spend. If nobody is already paying a human or a tool to solve this, the pain isn't budget-worthy yet.
  • No trigger pattern. If purchases don't cluster around identifiable events, there's no repeatable GTM motion to build.
  • Ingestion isn't the blocker. If RevOps says feeding a subledger is easy and the real problem is downstream judgment, Recurly has no structural advantage here.
08 · Willingness to Pay

Design the product around the price

Ramanujam and Tacke's argument in Monetizing Innovation is that most products fail commercially not because they're badly built but because the pricing conversation happens last. For a product that doesn't exist yet, that conversation is the first artifact, not the final one.

How I'd actually measure willingness to pay

Van Westendorp price sensitivity

Four questions - too expensive, expensive but worth considering, a bargain, so cheap you'd doubt it. Produces an acceptable range rather than a single number.

Gabor-Granger

Walk specific price points and measure purchase intent at each. Builds a demand curve and finds the revenue-maximizing point inside the Van Westendorp range.

Choice-based conjoint

Forced trade-offs between bundles at different prices. The only reliable way to separate leaders from fillers when everyone claims they want everything.

MaxDiff

Best-worst scaling across the capability list. Cheaper and faster than conjoint; good enough to rank the roadmap.

  • Deflate stated intent before it reaches the business case. "Definitely would buy" overstates real purchase behavior badly.
  • An undeflated top box is how a plausible model turns into a hockey stick nobody hits. Apply standard deflation factors or cross-validate against any existing transaction data.
The value story the sales team has to be able to tell: this doesn't cost X per year, it displaces Y reconciliation hours per close, returns Z days of close time, and removes a named category of audit exposure. If the buyer can't repeat that sentence back, the price is being defended against the software line item instead of the cost it replaces - and that argument is lost before it starts.
09 · Technical Feasibility

Don't build a second Zuora

  • Own what merchants judge Recurly on and what compounds over time: ingestion, lifecycle handling, audit trail, workflow
  • Partner for what's commodity infrastructure backed by network effects Recurly could never replicate: SSP logic, ERP connectors, tax
  • One precondition before any of this is sellable - a public ingestion contract that external systems can write to without knowing Recurly's internals
LayerCallWhy
Ingestion & canonical event modelOwnThe differentiation lives here - turning any source system's contract changes into lifecycle events
Workflow, dashboards, audit trailOwnWhere merchant trust is earned or lost; must be walkable by an external auditor
Novel SSP allocation logicPartnerGenuinely hard, genuinely commodity once solved - don't rebuild it from scratch
ERP connectors (NetSuite, Intacct, SAP)PartnerMature middleware already exists (Celigo, Boomi); rebuild-risk isn't worth it

The precondition nobody skips. RevRec today is presumably wired to Recurly Subscriptions' internal event bus. Before any of this is credible externally, it needs a public ingestion contract - a schema and API surface a CPQ system, a CSV export, or a NetSuite connector can write to without knowing Recurly's internals exist.
10 · Applied AI

Let the model find the exceptions. Let the controller decide.

Revenue recognition has a right answer, and the cost of getting it wrong is a restatement. Judgment stays with the controller. The model's job is narrower and more valuable: scan every contract event for discrepancies, surface the ones that need human attention, and store the reasoning so that when an auditor asks "why?" six months later, the answer takes seconds instead of days.

What the model does

Scans every contract event against the standards corpus, flags discrepancies, classifies exception type, and attaches the citation. Surfaces a prioritized queue so the controller sees what needs attention first.

What the model never does

Makes the call. The controller reviews, decides, and approves. The system records that decision with the supporting citation, the standard reference, and the reviewer identity so it can be retrieved instantly at audit.

Use case 1: RAG-assisted onboarding

Implementation is where challengers lose to incumbents. A new RevRec customer spends weeks with a consultant translating their contract portfolio and revenue recognition policy into system configuration - performance obligations, SSP methods, recognition patterns, lease treatment under IFRS 16 and ASC 842. That translation work is reading comprehension against a fixed body of standards. The model reads the contract, retrieves the governing paragraph from ASC 606 or IFRS 16, and proposes a treatment with the citation attached. The accountant reviews and approves - or overrides. Either way, the decision and its rationale are stored.

Onboarding assist pipeline

The model surfaces. The controller decides. The system remembers.

The human gate is the product, not a safety afterthought.
Use case 2: Exception dashboard for the controller

Every close cycle, the model scans all contract events against the recognition schedule and flags discrepancies - a mid-term seat change that was never re-allocated, a renewal whose pricing didn't chain to the prior schedule, a usage overage that crossed a tier boundary. Each exception lands in a dashboard with the contract reference, the specific variance, the applicable standard paragraph, and a suggested classification. The controller reviews the queue, makes the call, and the decision is logged with their identity and the supporting citation.

What the controller sees

Priority queue
Ranked by materiality
Highest-dollar and highest-risk exceptions surface first. The controller works a sorted, filterable queue, not a spreadsheet.
Each exception card
Contract + standard + variance
The contract clause, the governing ASC 606 or IFRS 16 paragraph, the dollar variance, and a suggested treatment. One click to approve, override, or escalate.
Decision log
Instant audit retrieval
Every decision stored with the citation, the reviewer, and the timestamp. When the auditor asks "why?" six months later, the controller pulls up the record in seconds.
Use case 3: Contract modification triage

Classifies a modification as separate contract, prospective, or cumulative catch-up, with the reasoning and governing paragraph attached. The most error-prone area of ASC 606. High value, higher stakes, so it ships after the eval harness is proven on the onboarding use case. The controller still makes the call; the model surfaces the classification and the evidence.

Corpus design - the part that decides whether this works
  • Two corpora, kept separate. The standards corpus (ASC 606, IFRS 15, IFRS 16, ASC 842, ASC 340-40, plus interpretive guidance) is shared. The customer corpus - their contracts, policy memo, historical schedules - is tenant-isolated and never crosses accounts.
  • Effective-dated and versioned. An auditor will ask which version of the guidance a decision was made under. The retrieval index carries effective dates, and a new ASU triggers re-indexing plus a full eval rerun - not a silent corpus swap.
  • Chunk at the paragraph level the standard itself uses. Citations have to land on something an accountant can look up. Arbitrary token windows produce citations nobody can verify, which is worse than no citation.
  • Retrieval failure must be visible. If nothing relevant comes back, the correct output is "no grounded answer, route to a human" - never an ungrounded generation. Silence is a valid response here.
Evals
  • Source design-partner contracts from Section 07 pilots, adjudicated by their own accountants
  • Pressure-tested against the auditor track before any AI assist ships
  • Held out, versioned, and re-run on every prompt change, model change, or corpus update - not a one-time exercise

Set the threshold from the cost ratio, not from a round accuracy number. A false flag costs an accountant four minutes of review. A missed exception costs a restatement, an audit finding, and the customer relationship. Those two errors are not within an order of magnitude of each other, so the confidence threshold gets tuned hard toward recall - and I'd resist committing to a headline accuracy figure before the golden set exists.
Rollout and guardrails
  • Every exception is human-reviewed. The model flags and ranks. The controller reviews and decides. There is no tier where the model makes a recognition decision on its own. Auto-apply is limited to low-judgment event types on a narrow whitelist, and only after measured accuracy on that specific type clears the bar.
  • Deterministic math, assisted triage. The model surfaces discrepancies and retrieves citations. The existing engine computes the schedule. An auditor can accept a rules engine doing arithmetic and a human making the judgment call. They cannot accept a language model doing either, and they shouldn't.
  • Every decision is logged as audit-ready evidence. The contract clause, the retrieved standard paragraph with corpus version, the model's suggested classification, the controller's actual decision, and the approver identity. When the auditor asks "why was this recognized this way?" the controller pulls up the full record - not a memory, not an email thread, not a spreadsheet version.
  • Override rate is the live production signal. If accountants start overriding more often, something has drifted - the corpus, the model, or the customer's contract mix. A better early warning than any offline metric.
  • Data quality is a hard ceiling. An agentic layer propagates bad inputs rather than catching them. If contract data is wrong upstream, no amount of retrieval quality fixes the schedule - which is the argument for owning ingestion in the first place.
Why this is a GTM lever, not a feature
  • Faster implementation RAG-assisted onboarding removes the biggest implementation-drag advantage incumbents hold
  • Lower cost to serve mid-market a segment that would otherwise be unprofitable to onboard at high-touch consulting rates
  • Direct value story displaced reconciliation headcount, recovered close-cycle time, instant audit retrieval instead of weeks of rework
  • Neither becomes a separate AI SKU charging for the thing that makes the product usable is the same mistake as metering the audit trail
11 · Go-to-Market

Land mid-market. Point at Salesforce. Don't start there.

Salesforce is the right north star and the wrong first customer. The ICP that gets this product proven is the segment that's outgrown Stripe RevRec but isn't ready for a multi-year Zuora implementation.

ICP v1

$10M–$500M ARR B2B SaaS with multi-element contracts, currently reconciling by hand or bolted onto a point tool like RightRev or Leapfin.

Entry motion

5–8 design partners, free pilot run against real historical contracts to prove schedule accuracy before any commercial conversation.

Channel

Existing Recurly enterprise relationships with adjacent B2B arms - a warmer path in than cold enterprise outbound.

Packaging

Sold standalone, not gated behind Recurly Subscriptions - the unbundling has to be true in the price sheet, not just the pitch.

Segment on willingness to pay, not company size
  • ARR band is a convenient proxy but a poor segmentation axis it says nothing about contract complexity or urgency
  • The real cut is contract complexity crossed with trigger urgency a $40M-ARR company three months from an S-1 has far higher willingness to pay than a $300M company with a clean audit history and a working spreadsheet
  • Segment on that and the pricing tiers follow the segments rather than the other way round
Sequencing the win story
  • Prove the model on the mid-market cohort first validate schedule accuracy and price with real design partners before any enterprise pitch
  • A Salesforce-shaped anchor logo becomes the reference story after proof exists not before - leading with the aspirational logo is how this pitch loses credibility with finance buyers
12 · Risks

Where I'd hold firm, and where I'd bend

Holding firm

  • The lifecycle-native wedge is real
  • Competing on Zuora's contract-depth terms is a losing bet - a decade head start doesn't close regardless of engineering investment

Open to evidence

  • If discovery shows SSP allocation is the actual blocker (not lifecycle handling), the build order flips
  • If no design partner has existing spend on this problem, the pain isn't budget-worthy yet
  • Cannibalization risk: a standalone RevRec could confuse or compete with the existing bundled RevRec offer - packaging has to draw a clear line.
  • Strategic fit risk: Recurly's core DNA is high-volume consumer subscription. If leadership decides B2B enterprise isn't the strategic direction, this doesn't get built regardless of market size - that call sits above this case study.
  • Timeline risk: the ingestion API precondition (Section 09) is real engineering time before any GTM motion is credible - not a fast follow.
  • Minivation risk: the pressure to price this as a cheap attach product to drive adoption is real, and it would permanently anchor the category at Stripe's rate. Easier to discount from a defensible price than to raise one.
  • AI credibility risk: one confidently wrong, well-cited recommendation in front of an auditor costs more trust than the feature ever earns. The eval harness is a precondition for shipping the assist, not a follow-on workstream - and if it isn't ready, the assist doesn't ship.
13 · Measurement

Four layers, and an honest note on lag

Merchant outcome
  • Schedule accuracy vs. prior manual process, per design partner
  • Days to close measured before and after; target is a measurable reduction within the first two close cycles
  • Time to live days from contract signed to the contract appearing in the subledger
Platform health
  • Contracts ingested per source system tracks whether multi-source ingestion is actually being used
  • Zero-touch rate share of lifecycle events reconciled with no manual journal entry
  • AI override rate if accountants start overriding more often, something has drifted - corpus, model, or contract mix
Commercial
  • Realized price vs. list discount depth signals whether the value story is landing with finance buyers
  • Win rate against the unbundlers specifically RightRev, Leapfin, Ordway - the most comparable alternatives
  • Implementation days per deal the GTM lever claim lives or dies here
Compliance risk - the lagging layer
  • Audit findings annual by nature; the leading proxy is exception rate and reconciliation variance
  • Restatement rate annual and lagging - name that gap up front rather than pretend it's a live metric
  • Override rate trend the best early warning signal that something has drifted before it reaches an audit
